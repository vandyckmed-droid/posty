    Method & caveats

    Universe

    Starts from every US-listed ETF on the vendor's master list, then applies one baseline liquidity screen so that everything ranked is actually tradeable:

    • Median daily dollar volume ≥ $5M over the last 63 sessions (median, not mean, so a single block trade can't lift a thin fund through the gate)
    • Last close ≥ $5
    • ≥ 273 sessions of price history, so the longest formation window exists

    Delisted tickers that still quote a stale last price are dropped automatically — they return no price history.

    Score

    Daily log returns rt = ln(Pt / Pt−1) on split- and dividend-adjusted closes, measured over a formation window that always stops 21 sessions short of today. That gap is the "−1", and it is there because one-month returns tend to reverse rather than persist.

    • 12−1 — trading days t−252 to t−21, 231 daily returns. The classic academic window; slower to turn, less prone to chasing a single hot quarter.
    • 6−1 — trading days t−126 to t−21, 105 daily returns. Reacts sooner to a trend that has only recently established itself, at the cost of a noisier estimate — half the observations means a wider error bar on every score.

    Both rank the same 1,058 funds, so nothing appears in one list and vanishes from the other. That does exclude 32 liquid funds young enough to have 6−1 history but not 12−1 — mostly single-stock geared products — in exchange for two rankings that are directly comparable. Every row's detail shows its rank on both windows with the gap between them: a fund at #6 on 12−1 and #140 on 6−1 is running on momentum that has already faded.

    1. Annualized return = Σr × 252 / n
    2. Annualized volatility = stdev(r) × √252
    3. Score = annualized return ÷ annualized volatility

    Both legs are measured on the same window and the same return series, so the score is a unitless figure: how much a fund out-earned cash per unit of the noise it made getting there. Returns are annualized in log space, which is what keeps the ratio internally consistent — but it also means a fund that fell 97% over the window reports a very large negative annualized figure. The total window return in each row's detail is the plain, compounded number.

    Why the T-bill return is netted out

    Dividing a raw return by volatility has one sharp consequence: T-bill funds win. On that measure SGOV scored 19.5 — 3.8% annualized return against 0.2% volatility — roughly six times the best real momentum name. Nothing was wrong with the arithmetic. A near-riskless asset simply has a near-zero denominator, and essentially all of that 3.8% was the risk-free rate.

    So the numerator is the return in excess of cash, using the 1–3 month T-bill fund's own return over the same window (). That makes the score a true Sharpe ratio and fixes the problem at its source rather than filtering around it: SGOV now scores near zero, correctly, because it beat cash by nothing. Above roughly 10% volatility the adjustment is invisible — subtracting 3.6% from a 200% annualized return moves the score by about 0.02.

    The funds with annualized volatility under 3% are still tagged cash-like, and the Hide row can still drop them, but they no longer need hiding to keep the ranking sane. A few genuinely earn their place — an ultra-short credit fund that reliably clears T-bills has a real information ratio, just an unlevered and very small one. The Raw score in each row's detail, and as a sort, is the un-netted ratio.

    How much to trust a score

    A score is an estimate from a sample of daily returns, not a fact about the fund, and its sampling error scales with 1/√n. Each row's detail carries a 90% confidence interval from the standard Sharpe-ratio standard error, √252 · √((1 + s²/2) / n). It is wide: roughly ±1.7 on 12−1 and ±2.6 on 6−1, against a top-of-list spread of a few tenths. Gaps under about 0.1 are noise, and the shorter window buys its faster reaction with a materially noisier estimate.

    One important exception. That interval describes the level of a single score. Comparisons between two correlated funds are far sharper, because what matters there is the error on their difference, and highly correlated funds barely differ. SOXX and SOXQ, correlated 0.997, can be ordered with about 87% confidence off a gap of 0.09 — a gap the intervals above would call meaningless. Trust the ranking most within a group, least across the whole list.

    Grouping similar funds

    A score ranks funds, but what you can actually hold is bets, and the two come apart badly at the top of a momentum screen. SOXX, SOXQ and SMH have pairwise return correlations of 0.986 to 0.997. Owning all three is one position at triple size.

    So the screen folds any fund that moves with a better-ranked one into that fund's row. There are two ideas of "moves with", and the Group row picks between them:

    • Same fund — raw return correlation above 0.95. Catches index twins: different wrappers around the same holdings.
    • Same bet — correlation above after removing the market. Catches a theme held through different funds, issuers and gearing. This is the default.

    The market being removed is not a chosen benchmark. It is the single strongest pattern of shared movement in this window's own data — the first principal component, worth of all variance across the 1,058 funds. Using the data's own common factor rather than an equity index matters here, because plenty of these funds are not equities: measured this way, Treasury funds cluster with Treasury funds and bitcoin funds with bitcoin funds, without anything being told what asset class it belongs to.

    The difference is stark. On raw correlation, semiconductors and large-cap value sit at 0.52 — high enough to look related, but only because both are equities. Remove the market and they fall to −0.18: unrelated, correctly. Semiconductors against each other barely move, 0.99 to 0.98. That separation is exactly what a raw correlation cannot make at any threshold, which is why an earlier attempt at simply loosening the raw bar to 0.80 had a value fund swallowing all three semiconductor funds.

    Membership is always measured against the representative, never chained through intermediates, so a group cannot drift into a different bet. Tap the summary line on any row to see what was folded in and how closely it tracks. Grouping never removes a fund from the ranking — it nests it, and each row's detail still reports its true rank across all funds. Correlations are recomputed per window, so switching to 12−1 re-forms the groups.

    One caution the group list makes visible: correlation ignores scale, so a geared fund is near-perfectly correlated with its own parent — SOXL and SOXX sit at 0.995. They are the same bet, but they are not substitutes, so every member shows its own annualized volatility beside the correlation. Same direction, very different position size.

    Independent bets

    The third readout answers: if you bought the top rows shown in equal size, how many genuinely separate positions would that be? It is N² / ΣΣ|rij| on raw correlations — the count of unrelated holdings carrying the same risk as the basket, and raw is right here because raw is what your money actually experiences. Ten uncorrelated funds score 10; ten copies of one fund score 1. Correlations enter as absolute values on purpose: an inverse fund is a perfect hedge against its own long, and that pair is one bet expressed twice, not a free lunch.

    It sits near 2, and no grouping setting moves it much — market-adjusted grouping included. That is worth stating plainly, because it is the finding rather than a gap in the tool. Folding away duplicates changes which ten funds you see, not how much they overlap; when a near-twin is folded away, the fund promoted from beneath it tends to be about as correlated with the rest. The top of a momentum screen is genuinely one or two trades wearing ten tickers, and that is a fact about the market in this window, not an artifact to engineer away.

    Which makes the two readouts a useful pair. Same bet grouping tells you what you are really choosing between. The bets figure tells you that choosing more rows from the top will not, by itself, diversify you.

    Reading a row

    The bar under each ticker places the score on a shared diverging scale centred on zero, so relative standing is visible without reading the number. Scores beyond the scale are clamped to its ends. Tap any row for the full window detail.

    Snapshot, not a live feed. Every figure is fixed at the close shown in the header and will not update. Leveraged and inverse products are ranked alongside everything else because they clear the same liquidity bar — their daily-reset compounding makes a multi-month score mean something different, so the Hide row lets you drop them. Momentum rank is a starting screen, not a recommendation, and says nothing about what happens next.
