    Method & caveats

    Universe

    Starts from every US-listed ETF on the vendor's master list, then applies one baseline liquidity screen so that everything ranked is actually tradeable:

    • Median daily dollar volume ≥ $5M over the last 63 sessions (median, not mean, so a single block trade can't lift a thin fund through the gate)
    • Last close ≥ $5
    • ≥ 273 sessions of price history, so the longest formation window exists

    Delisted tickers that still quote a stale last price are dropped automatically — they return no price history.

    Score

    Daily log returns rt = ln(Pt / Pt−1) on split- and dividend-adjusted closes, measured over a formation window that always stops 21 sessions short of today. That gap is the "−1", and it is there because one-month returns tend to reverse rather than persist.

    • 12−1 — trading days t−252 to t−21, 231 daily returns. The classic academic window; slower to turn, less prone to chasing a single hot quarter.
    • 6−1 — trading days t−126 to t−21, 105 daily returns. Reacts sooner to a trend that has only recently established itself, at the cost of a noisier estimate — half the observations means a wider error bar on every score.

    Both rank the same 1,058 funds, so nothing appears in one list and vanishes from the other. That does exclude 32 liquid funds young enough to have 6−1 history but not 12−1 — mostly single-stock geared products — in exchange for two rankings that are directly comparable. Every row's detail shows its rank on both windows with the gap between them: a fund at #6 on 12−1 and #140 on 6−1 is running on momentum that has already faded.

    1. Annualized return = Σr × 252 / n
    2. Annualized volatility = stdev(r) × √252
    3. Score = annualized return ÷ annualized volatility

    Both legs are measured on the same window and the same return series, so the score is a unitless figure: how much trend a fund delivered per unit of the noise it made getting there. Returns are annualized in log space, which is what keeps the ratio internally consistent — but it also means a fund that fell 97% over the window reports a very large negative annualized figure. The total window return in each row's detail is the plain, compounded number.

    Why cash-like funds are hidden by default

    No risk-free rate is subtracted, so this is a raw return/volatility ratio, not a Sharpe ratio — and that has one sharp consequence: T-bill funds win. SGOV returned 3.8% annualized against 0.2% volatility, scoring 19.5, roughly six times the best real momentum name. Nothing is wrong with the arithmetic; a near-riskless asset simply has a near-zero denominator.

    Rather than change the formula, the screen tags the funds whose annualized volatility is under 3% as cash-like and hides them by default. Tap Cash-like in the Hide row to put them back — their true rank across all funds is always shown in each row's detail. Sorting by Excess score applies the other fix, netting the T-bill return (, measured on the same window) out of the numerator before dividing.

    Grouping near-identical funds

    A score ranks funds, but what you can actually hold is bets, and the two come apart badly at the top of a momentum screen. SOXX, SOXQ and SMH have pairwise return correlations of 0.986 to 0.997; strip out their market beta and the residuals are still correlated 0.97+. Owning all three is one position at triple size.

    So the screen correlates every pair of daily return series over the active formation window — switch to 6−1 and the groups re-form on 6−1 correlations — and folds any fund correlated with a better-ranked one above the threshold into that fund's row. Membership is measured against the representative, not chained through intermediates, so a group never drifts into a different bet. Tap n near-identical funds on any row to see what was folded in and at what correlation.

    One caution the group list makes visible: correlation is scale-invariant, so a geared fund is near-perfectly correlated with its own parent — SOXL and SOXX sit at 0.998. They are the same bet, but they are not substitutes, so each member shows its own annualized volatility next to the correlation. Same direction, very different position size.

    Grouping never removes a fund from the ranking — it nests it, and each row's detail still reports its true rank across all funds. Turn it Off for the flat list, or loosen to r ≥ 0.90 to fold tight sector clusters rather than just index twins.

    Independent bets

    The third readout answers: if you bought the top rows shown in equal size, how many genuinely separate positions would that be? It is N² / ΣΣ|rij| — the count of unrelated holdings carrying the same risk as the basket. Ten uncorrelated funds score 10; ten copies of one fund score 1. Correlations enter as absolute values on purpose: an inverse fund is a perfect hedge against its own long, and that pair is one bet expressed twice, not a free lunch.

    It sits near 2 here, and grouping barely moves it. That is not a bug in either feature — it is the finding. Folding away near-twins removes funds that are the same holding, but the leaders' remaining commonality is a shared factor: the large-cap value block correlates about 0.79 internally, and market beta puts most equity funds at 0.7–0.9 with each other regardless of what they own. A top 10 of ten distinct tickers is still roughly two trades.

    Loosening the threshold would not fix it either, and the screen deliberately stops at 0.90. At r ≥ 0.80 the value factor fund absorbs 44 others — including all three semiconductor funds, which correlate 0.83–0.85 with it purely as equities. That is a worse answer, not a looser one: separating a sector bet from plain market exposure needs correlation measured on market-adjusted returns, not on raw returns at a lower bar.

    Reading a row

    The bar under each ticker places the score on a shared diverging scale centred on zero, so relative standing is visible without reading the number. Scores beyond the scale are clamped to its ends. Tap any row for the full window detail.

    Snapshot, not a live feed. Every figure is fixed at the close shown in the header and will not update. Leveraged and inverse products are ranked alongside everything else because they clear the same liquidity bar — their daily-reset compounding makes a 12-month score mean something different, so the filters above let you drop them. Momentum rank is a starting screen, not a recommendation, and says nothing about what happens next.
